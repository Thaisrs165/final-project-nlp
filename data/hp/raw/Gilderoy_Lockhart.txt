Personality and traits
Personality and traits
— Lockhart displaying his arrogance as he talks to Harry
Gilderoy Lockhart's defining characteristics were his extreme arrogance, vanity, conceit, egotism and self-obsession.1]2]3] From childhood, he possessed an intense desire to be exceptional and famous, having been raised by a mother who regarded him as extraordinary and consequently arriving at Hogwarts expecting his fellow students and teachers to be similarly impressed by him.4] He was greatly disappointed to discover that he was neither uniquely talented nor the centre of attention he had expected to be. Although his teachers considered him to possess above-average intelligence and magical ability, and he achieved good marks, Lockhart increasingly valued learning principally for the recognition it could bring him. He developed extravagant ambitions, including creating a Philosopher's Stone before graduation, captaining the English National Quidditch team to World Cup victory and becoming Britain's youngest Minister for Magic, but was unwilling to apply himself consistently unless he believed he could be the best at what he attempted. If unable to distinguish himself through genuine achievement, he increasingly preferred shortcuts, attention-seeking schemes and other means of obtaining recognition.4]
Even as a student, Lockhart's desire for attention frequently manifested itself in grandiose and disruptive displays. Among other schemes, he carved his signature into the Quidditch pitch in enormous letters, sent himself hundreds of Valentine's cards which caused an outbreak of owl droppings at breakfast, and devised a spell which projected an enormous illuminated image of his own face into the sky.4] These antics earned him notoriety rather than the widespread admiration he desired, and he was never particularly popular at school. By the time he graduated, much of the Hogwarts staff were relieved to see him leave.4] His failure to achieve his extraordinary youthful ambitions did not extinguish his desire for fame; instead, his unwillingness to accept being merely above average increasingly led him to seek fraudulent means of becoming the exceptional wizard he believed he deserved to be.4]
After leaving Hogwarts, Lockhart discovered a particularly effective means of obtaining the fame he desired. He travelled widely and tracked down accomplished witches and wizards who had performed extraordinary deeds, persuaded them to explain exactly how they had accomplished them, and then used his considerable proficiency with Memory Charms to modify their recollections before publishing their experiences as his own.3]4] His fraud therefore involved considerably more planning and effort than simply inventing heroic stories: he deliberately sought out genuine achievements, acquired detailed information from the people responsible for them, removed their ability to contradict his version of events and reconstructed their adventures with himself as the hero, supplementing them with a wealth of invented detail.4] This demonstrated considerable cunning, resourcefulness and ruthlessness, as Lockhart was willing to permanently interfere with the memories of innocent people solely to acquire recognition for their accomplishments.
Lockhart proved exceptionally successful at cultivating the resulting public image. His natural good looks, charisma, writing ability and talent for self-promotion complemented the heroic persona presented in his books, and within a decade he had become a bestselling author regarded as a world-class defender against the Dark Arts.4] He possessed a shrewd understanding of celebrity and of what appealed to his audience, openly recognising that the genuine heroes whose achievements he appropriated were less commercially marketable than the handsome and charismatic figure he could present to readers.3] He was also highly opportunistic in exploiting publicity. When he encountered the already famous Harry Potter at his book signing in Flourish and Blotts, Lockhart immediately drew Harry into the assembled photographers' attention and used the occasion to announce his appointment as Defence Against the Dark Arts professor.1] After the subsequent fight between Arthur Weasley and Lucius Malfoy, he even attempted to persuade the Daily Prophet photographer to incorporate the incident into the report of his signing, regarding virtually any public attention as useful publicity.1]
Despite his considerable understanding of publicity and mass appeal, Lockhart was much less perceptive regarding the immediate reactions of people around him. He frequently interpreted discomfort, irritation or outright dislike as admiration, and appeared largely oblivious to the extent to which his behaviour annoyed those who were not already among his admirers. When Ronald Weasley reacted with disgust to Lockhart's ostentatious peacock-feather quill, for example, Lockhart assumed that Ron was admiring it, while Harry's repeated discomfort with Lockhart's attention did little to dissuade him from believing that Harry welcomed his advice about fame.2] He likewise failed to appreciate how strongly his habit of offering unsolicited professional advice irritated his fellow members of staff. Lockhart advised Pomona Sprout on treating the injured Whomping Willow, repeatedly offered Rubeus Hagrid advice on matters involving magical creatures, and claimed extensive experience brewing Mandrake Restorative Draught, prompting Severus Snape to remind him pointedly that Snape was the school's Potions Master.2]5]
This disparity between Lockhart's skill at appealing to a distant audience and his poor ability to judge people who knew him personally had been apparent since his school years. Lockhart had been unpopular among those who experienced his attention-seeking behaviour first-hand, yet his later public persona proved convincing enough that reports of his apparent courage and resilience caused several of his former teachers to wonder whether they had underestimated him.4] Albus Dumbledore, however, knew two of the people whose achievements Lockhart had appropriated and suspected the truth. Dumbledore consequently believed that bringing Lockhart back into an ordinary school environment would expose his fraud, as sustained personal scrutiny would require him to demonstrate the abilities and practical experience which he could more easily claim through books, photographs and publicity appearances.4] Minerva McGonagall, who had never been impressed by Lockhart, similarly remained largely immune to his celebrity.4]
Lockhart was nevertheless capable of considerable social manipulation when he understood what motivated the person with whom he was dealing. His vanity also made him particularly susceptible to the same tactic. Hermione Granger was able to obtain his signature on a permission slip for a book in the Hogwarts Library's Restricted Section by flattering him and appealing to his supposed expertise; Lockhart was so pleased by her admiration that he signed the note without properly examining the book she intended to borrow.6] Harry and Ron later exploited the same weakness to distract him from his responsibilities by encouraging him to attend to his appearance. His eagerness to receive admiration could therefore override his judgement even when greater caution or professionalism was required.
Lockhart's vanity was accompanied by considerable arrogance and a tendency to exaggerate his own importance and expertise. He regularly referred to his supposed adventures when offering advice and frequently implied that he could have handled situations better than the people actually responsible for them. When Mrs Norris was found apparently lifeless and rigid, Lockhart confidently suggested that she had been subjected to the Transmogrifian Torture, claimed familiarity with the curse and its counter-curse, and recounted an allegedly similar incident from his travels.5] Although his diagnosis was incorrect, his fraudulent history does not establish that the curse or the underlying magical knowledge he invoked was itself fictitious; Lockhart's characteristic deception was principally to represent knowledge and achievements acquired from others as products of his own extensive personal experience. Similarly, although his celebrated defeat of the Wagga Wagga Werewolf had actually been accomplished by another wizard, Lockhart was genuinely capable of performing the Homorphus Charm associated with the story and later demonstrated the spell publicly.7]
— Lockhart foolishly underestimating Snape's far superior duelling skills
Lockhart's arrogance could also cause him to badly underestimate other witches and wizards. While conducting the Duelling Club, he patronisingly introduced Snape as possessing only a small amount of duelling experience and assured the students that their Potions professor would survive their demonstration.8] Snape immediately defeated him with a Disarming Charm. Lockhart rapidly attempted to preserve his dignity by claiming that he had allowed himself to be struck in order to demonstrate the consequences of the spell, illustrating both his reluctance to acknowledge failure and his ability to improvise self-serving explanations when his public persona was threatened.8] His confidence in his own abilities therefore frequently exceeded his actual contemporary proficiency, particularly after years spent neglecting most forms of practical magic in favour of perfecting Memory Charms.
Lockhart was not, however, inherently devoid of magical talent or intelligence. His Hogwarts teachers had regarded him as above average in both respects, and he had once possessed reasonably good magical skills.4] His principal failing was his unwillingness to develop those abilities through sustained effort when genuine accomplishment offered less immediate recognition than shortcuts and self-promotion. Over years of concentrating almost exclusively upon Memory Charms while allowing his other abilities to lapse, his broader practical skills became rusty almost beyond repair.4] Consequently, by the time he returned to Hogwarts as a professor, there was an enormous disparity between the exceptional practical mastery attributed to the hero of his books and Lockhart's actual contemporary abilities. His failures with the Cornish Pixies, in duelling and in performing healing magic consequently exposed limitations which his carefully controlled public appearances had previously allowed him to conceal.2]6]8]
Lockhart was also highly impractical and could be reckless when his desire to appear knowledgeable or important overrode his judgement. His first practical Defence Against the Dark Arts lesson ended disastrously when he released a cage of Cornish Pixies and proved unable to regain control of them.2] When Harry's arm was broken during a Quidditch match, Lockhart ignored Harry's objections and attempted to mend it himself, only to incorrectly perform Brackium Emendo and vanish the bones from Harry's arm.6] The incident demonstrated the deterioration of his practical magical proficiency rather than establishing that he had never understood the spell or its intended purpose. Likewise, during the Duelling Club demonstration he used Alarte Ascendare to launch a conjured snake into the air, an action which did little to resolve the danger despite the spell itself functioning successfully.8] His poor judgement therefore frequently compounded the deterioration of his practical skills.
His tenure at Hogwarts also demonstrated that Lockhart's talent for presenting himself as an expert did not translate into effective teaching. Much of his instruction revolved around his own published adventures, with students reading his books, answering questions about Lockhart himself and theatrically reenacting scenes from his supposed exploits.2]6] This approach reflected both his vanity and his dependence upon a carefully constructed autobiographical persona. Although he possessed genuine magical knowledge and taught some practical defensive magic, his classes increasingly exposed the difference between being able to recount or write convincingly about dangerous encounters and possessing the practical experience necessary to respond effectively when confronted with comparable situations himself.
Lockhart's self-importance also manifested itself in his response to the reopening of the Chamber of Secrets. He repeatedly claimed that he understood the attacks better than his colleagues, offered confident explanations for events and insisted that he could have handled the situation more effectively. Nevertheless, despite his tendency to dismiss the danger and exaggerate his ability to deal with it, Lockhart did participate in the protective measures imposed by the school. When teachers were assigned to escort students between lessons, he took his turn doing so, and on one occasion appeared dishevelled after apparently spending most of the previous night patrolling the fourth floor.3] Characteristically, he simultaneously complained that such precautions were unnecessary and continued to insist that the danger had already passed. His willingness to perform routine security duties therefore contrasted sharply with his reaction when his claims of personal expertise were finally put to the test. After Ginny Weasley was taken into the Chamber, his colleagues reminded him of his repeated assertions that he knew where the Chamber was, understood what was inside it and could have dealt with the monster himself. Faced with the prospect of actually confronting it, Lockhart abandoned his pretence and attempted to flee the school.3]
This revealed one of the sharpest contrasts between Lockhart's public persona and his actual character. Although he had built his career around stories portraying himself as extraordinarily courageous, he was fundamentally cowardly when confronted with genuine mortal danger. Once Harry and Ron discovered him packing to escape Hogwarts, Lockhart admitted that the accomplishments in his books belonged to other people and showed little willingness to risk himself to rescue Ginny.3] His cowardice was accompanied by profound selfishness and a willingness to harm others to protect himself. After Harry and Ron discovered the entrance to the Chamber and forced him to accompany them, Lockhart seized Ron's damaged wand and attempted to erase both boys' memories, intending to leave Ginny to her fate and return with a fabricated account in which he had been unable to save her and Harry and Ron had tragically lost their minds.3] This willingness to destroy the memories of children and abandon another child to die in order to preserve his reputation demonstrated that beneath Lockhart's comical vanity lay a capacity for calculated and serious wrongdoing.
— An Amnesiac Lockhart demonstrating his remaining vanity and extravagance
His attempt failed when Ron's damaged wand caused the Memory Charm to backfire, destroying Lockhart's own memories instead.3] His demeanour changed noticeably following his memory loss. He appeared vague and easily distracted, reacted with delighted amazement to the magic he encountered, and showed none of his former need to insist upon his own accomplishments: when Dumbledore's conversation revealed that he had been a professor, Lockhart reacted with mild surprise and readily supposed that he had probably been a poor one.9] Following his permanent admission to St Mungo's Hospital for Magical Maladies and Injuries, he remained similarly good-natured when encountered by Harry, Ron, Hermione and Ginny several years later. Nevertheless, some aspects of his former personality persisted despite his amnesia. Lockhart remained delighted by attention, continued to enjoy signing autographs and took considerable pleasure in receiving fan mail, even though he no longer remembered why anybody wanted his autograph in the first place.10] His enduring attraction to recognition suggested that the desire to be noticed, unlike the grandiose self-image and fraudulent identity he had constructed around it, was deeply ingrained in his personality.
